Open Fly put one fruit-fly brain in front of a strategy game. This is the sequel nobody asked the animals about: seven mapped brains, from a 302-neuron roundworm to a zebrafish larva, take seats in the same world of Open Doctrines and play against each other. A mouse's visual cortex watches.

Now the part that is true. Every brain here is a simplified model built on a published wiring diagram. It runs on your machine, turn after turn, and every order its country gives comes from its spikes. Nothing is pre-recorded, and nothing was trained.

Who plays

  • Fruit fly, adult female: 138,639 neurons (FlyWire). Open Fly's brain, spike for spike.
  • Fruit fly, adult male: 166,700 neurons (MaleCNS v1.0), with the female's mapping and constants.
  • Zebrafish larva, 7 days old: 187,052 cell bodies (Fish1).
  • Sea squirt larva: 207 neurons, with its gap junctions (Ryan et al. 2016).
  • Roundworm, hermaphrodite: 302 neurons, with gap junctions (Cook et al. 2019). Local only.
  • Roundworm, male: 385 neurons, with gap junctions (Cook et al. 2019). Local only.
  • Fruit fly, larva: 2,952 neurons (Winding et al. 2023). Local only.

Local only means this page cannot serve it. The two worms and the fly larva come from datasets published with no licence that allows us to redistribute them. The page lists them and says why. They play in a copy you build and run yourself from the published data.

How a brain plays a strategy game

It does not know it is playing one. Each turn, what happened to its country becomes four numbers, worked out the same way for every animal:

  • Reward: land gained, and a budget in surplus.
  • Harm: land lost, a budget in deficit, and new wars.
  • Reserve: money in the treasury.
  • Threat: how many wars it is fighting.

Each number drives a group of that animal's own sensory neurons, named from its dataset's annotations. For the adult flies these are sugar, bitter and water taste neurons and the antenna's Johnston's organ. For the worm they are chemosensory, nociceptive, oxygen and touch neurons. Then the brain runs for 200 milliseconds of simulated time. Its motor set (descending neurons in the flies, motor neurons in the worms and the sea squirt) is dealt into 39 groups, one for each action the game offers, and the groups that fire most decide what the country does.

That mapping is ours, not the animals'. It was written down before each species played a single turn. Where a dataset was missing something, we filled the gap with a stated convention, and the page shows it as a warning beside the animal. Two examples: the zebrafish senses through four brain nuclei, because its sense organs are not yet connected in the traced data, and the fly larva's sensory neurons are assumed to be excitatory.

A stage with two animals compares two mappings

A stage with two animals on it compares two mappings we wrote, not two animals. If a fly outlasts a worm, that says something about the way we turned a fly's senses and neurons into a game, and almost nothing about flies and worms. So the page never announces that one species beat another.

The fair opponent is the same brain with its wiring shuffled: the same neurons, with the connections between them moved at random, set to be about as active as the real one. Any animal can play against its shuffled self. With its real wiring the fly is more active and more aggressive than with shuffled wiring, and against the game's AI that loses land.

The mouse watches

The mouse does not play. Its dataset, MICrONS, is a cubic millimetre of visual cortex: beautiful, and with no motor output, because it is not a whole brain. Seating it would mean supplying the rest of the animal ourselves.

So the mouse does what the data can do. Each turn the world map is shown to the MICrONS digital twin, a published model trained on recordings from the same mouse whose cortex was later reconstructed. The page draws what the model predicts 8,221 of that mouse's recorded neurons would do. You can also look at the room through the mouse's eyes: low resolution and two colours, green and ultraviolet. That view is a simulation of its optics, not a recording.

What it is not

No animals were harmed, because nothing here is alive. None of these models learned to play, none knows it is playing, and none of them can feel anything. They are simplifications: leaky integrate-and-fire neurons on wiring diagrams, several of them incomplete. The competence of any seat comes mostly from our mapping, and the page says so.

Pick the world

  • Choose who sits down, how many (up to eight seats), and whether each one has its own wiring or a shuffled copy.
  • Choose any map that ships with Open Doctrines, or import your own .odmap from the game's map editor.
  • Every animal plays the same 120 turns in the same world.

Play it yourself

Open Doctrines is a free, source-available grand strategy game that runs in your browser, with a map editor and multiplayer. If you only want the fly, Open Fly is the one-animal version this page grew out of. You will almost certainly do better than any of them.
